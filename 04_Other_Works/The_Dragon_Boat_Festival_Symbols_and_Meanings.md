The Dragon Boat Festival, Symbols and Meanings

Anyone who has ever encountered the enigmatic, mysterious China has probably heard of the Dragon Boat Festival.

Brief Background

This festival is held on the fifth day of the fifth month of the lunar calendar and is associated with the memory of Qu Yuan, a great poet who could not bear the fall of the Chu kingdom—to which he remained loyal despite persecution—and threw himself into a river.
Upon learning this, local residents rushed out in their boats, hoping to save him, but, unfortunately, even his body was never found. Peasants beat the water with their oars and threw rice into the river, trying to scare off or feed the fish and dragons so that they would not touch the poet's body.

It is believed that the dragon boat races, the pyramid-shaped rice dumplings called zongzi, and various traditional protective amulets associated with the festival are all linked to these events. It is also clearly emphasized that this festival stretches far back into antiquity, and that the mournful event related to Qu Yuan was superimposed on much older traditions connected to the mythical Yu the Great, the conqueror of the great flood and founder of the first dynasty, the Xia.

Moreover, during the fifth lunar month—the summer solstice period—the Dragon constellation rises to its highest point in the sky. The Dragon reigns in the heavens and is as close as possible to people, ready to hear their prayers. From this perspective, the festival is considered an ancient ritual of worship for the dragon totem responsible for rainfall. In some regions of China, the boat races are specifically held in honor of the lord and deity of the waters, Yu the Great, symbolizing the celestial movement of the dragon to ensure safety on the rivers for the entire year.

Please note: the following reflections are purely subjective and do not claim scientific accuracy.

But why, in his moment of despair, did Qu Yuan choose the deep water? It's difficult to answer definitively. Let's turn to one of his poems. In his poem "Heavenly Questions", there is an entire section dedicated to a mythical figure with whom the poet felt a profound spiritual connection as a fellow outcast and sufferer for justice. This figure is Gun.
According to Yuan Ke's book "Myths of Ancient China", the mythical Gun was one of the supreme celestial deities, possibly even the grandson of the Heavenly Emperor. He was depicted as a white horse.

This was during the great flood that tormented the people. Among all the deities, only Gun felt deep compassion for humanity and decided to help them. He stole the most valuable possession of his grandfather — the Xirang. Xirang is a small lump of earth that could expand without limits. With it, he planned to build dams and create land for the people. But the Supreme Emperor learned of the theft. Gun was severely punished and executed, and the flooding regained its devastating power and strength.

Yet a myth would not be a myth if the story ended there. The people suffered immeasurably, and the soul of Gun, who had loved them so deeply, did not leave his body. It did not decompose for three years, because in order to complete his work, Gun nurtured a new life within himself. This was his son, Yu — a dragon with a pair of strong, sharp horns. And as we already know, Yu succeeded in taming the great flood.

When Yu was born, Gun either transformed into a bear and departed, as stated in a commentary to the "Classic of Mountains and Seas" (though the main text says, "Here the father of Yu transformed"), or he plunged into the depths as a three-legged turtle, a black fish, or a yellow dragon. But... the depths...

In his "Heavenly Questions", Qu Yuan asks about justice: why was Yu allowed to battle the flood, while Gun was punished?

"Gun strove, but failed to tame the currents!
Why was he hindered from applying his great experience?
For the giant turtle and the owls, through their sorcerous games,
Only ruined Gun's work!..
For what was the hero executed by the Lord?"

It should be noted here that this part of the myth coincides with the Biblical flood in its underlying message: people on earth had stopped believing in a righteous path, committed evils, and thereby incurred divine wrath. As a warning, the Heavenly Emperor sent a great flood upon them. Of course, this is just a supposition, but such a parallel gives one pause.


Perhaps Gun entered the struggle immediately, defying the Emperor's will. Yu, on the other hand, entered the struggle three years later. According to one version, the Heavenly Emperor was frightened by Yu's miraculous birth; according to another, his heart had finally softened. After all, the Biblical flood was not eternal either, and it ended after one year and several months.

But Gun is, for Qu Yuan, a mirror of his own suffering: to fight for justice, to be faithful to duty, but to be rejected. Of course, this is only a hypothesis, but perhaps it is exactly for this reason that Qu Yuan, seeing the reflection of his own fate in Gun, chose the dark water.

Why specifically on the fifth day of the fifth month of the lunar calendar? The official story is that it was a ritual protest to awaken the conscience of the Chu king and foster national unity. But it is also worth noting something else: Qu Yuan chose not just death, but death by drowning—immersion in water, the very element of Gun. And the day he chose was the day when the dragon is closest to people. Perhaps these are not random coincidences, but a deeply deliberate symbolic act: the poet wanted to show that he shared the fate of the one whose work was completed only after his death. And so it happened! For he went into the depths like Gun, giving his whole soul to his people so that a new power might be born—one like Yu. A power capable of uniting the people and saving the kingdom.

Now, let's turn to the symbols of the festival.

First, the dragon boat races. Having received permission and the Xirang from the Heavenly Emperor, Yu began his war on the great flood. With him, the Heavenly Emperor's own dragon, Yinglong, descended from the heavens, along with other great and lesser dragons.

What did the dragons do? They laid out paths for the water. Yinglong carved the main channels, while the other dragons carved the tributaries, all the way to the Eastern Sea. Humans and dragons worked together. Is this not a perfect symbol for the dragon boat races? Imagine it: the dragons are charting the course, the people are digging the canals, and they are all striving together toward the sea. Dragon boats full of people, competing for speed, racing toward the finish! The winning boat is Yinglong, and its rowers are Yu the Great! All the boats behind are the other great and lesser dragons and the people who dug the canals. If you look at the race from this perspective, there are no losers—everyone is an equal participant; the event merely determines who is Yinglong and who is Yu. It is, in effect, a mythological reconstruction of the many long years of work spent building canals to channel away the waters!

And so, Yinglong and Yu the Great are chosen.
Next, the zongzi. These could very well be the Xirang! The unique property of this treasure was its ability to expand many times over. This sounds remarkably similar to cooked rice. It's possible that the pyramid-shaped rice dumplings are a symbolic representation of the Xirang. Using Xirang, embankments and dams were raised, which, due to their magical properties, did not erode but instead grew, turning into entire mountain ridges. The Xirang was a most significant element in the fight against the flood.

The Xirang is wrapped in either bamboo or reed leaves. In Yuan Ke's book, it is said that to prepare for the battle against Gonggong, the water deity who controlled the flood, Yu the Great assembled all the gods on Mount Guiji. According to legend, the mountain was formerly called Reed Mountain. It was on this very mountain that the decisions regarding the methods for subduing Gonggong were made. Thus, everything in the Xirang—from the rice to the leaf—seems to be filled with profound meaning.

And, of course, the amulets. After the flood was quelled, Yu was greatly revered by his people, and the Emperor Shun handed down the rule to Yu for his great merits. After Yu became emperor, he cast nine massive cauldrons inscribed with images of all the malevolent spirits and evil creatures he had encountered during his long years of struggling against the water element. This was done so that people who saw these images could recognize danger in time and be more cautious. The myths also state that people embarked on journeys carrying amulets with them. Yu the Great strove to ensure that his people were safe. Perhaps that is why, on this day, it is customary to wear protective amulets and hang herbs over doorways to protect the home.

There is one more interesting detail, but one must be extremely careful in assessing its potential impact. Yu the Great was the first emperor to found a dynasty (the Xia). Previously, power was passed to the wisest or most capable. After Yu the Great, his son Qi became the next emperor. And this was due to the will of the people, who saw him as the heir of the people's savior from the great flood. Thus, imperial power gained hereditary succession.

Let's remember that Yu the Great was born a dragon, with strong, sharp horns. Perhaps this is exactly why the horned dragon became the symbol of imperial authority. The five-clawed, horned dragon was forbidden for depiction to anyone except the emperor.

What are horns in nature? Horns help animals assert their priority and authority in battle. Therefore, the horned dragon can serve not only as a symbol of the emperor as the supreme ruler but also as the symbol of the very first dragon who became an emperor—Yu the Great himself.

And so it is that the Dragon Boat Festival is both a symbol of patriotism and a deep memory of sacrifice and selflessness for the good of the people.
